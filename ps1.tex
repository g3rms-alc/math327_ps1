\section*{Question 1}
Suppose \(n \in \ZZ[]^+\). We prove by induction.\\
Suppose \(P(n)\) is \(169 \mid 3^{3k+3} - 26k -27\). We prove the base case
when \(n=1\). Suppose \(n=1\), Then,
\begin{align*}
    3^{3n +3} -26n -27 & = 3^{3(1) +3} -26(1) -27 \\
                       & =3^6 - 27 -27            \\
                       & =729 -53                 \\
                       & =676                     \\
                       & = 4 \cdot 169
\end{align*}
We obtain \(169 \mid 676\). So \(P(1)\) is true.
\\[0.25cm]
Then suppose \(a_n = 3^{3n+3}-26b-27\). Suppose \(169 \mid a_n\) for some
fixed integer n. Then,
\begin{align*}
    a_{n+1} - a_n & = (3^{3n + 6} - 26(n+1) - 27 ) - (3^{3n +3} -26n -27) \\
                  & = 3^{3n + 6} - 3^{3n+3} -26                           \\
                  & = 3^{3n}\cdot 3^3 \cdot 3^3 - 3^{3n +3} - 26          \\
                  & = 27 \cdot 3^{3n +3} - 3^{3n + 3} -26                 \\
                  & = 3^{3n + 3}(27 -1) - 26 = 3^{3n + 3}(3^3 -1) - 26    \\
                  & = 3^{3n + 3}\cdot26 - 26                              \\
                  & = 26(3^{3n + 3}-1) = 2\cdot13(3^{3n + 3} -1)
\end{align*}
We need \(169 \mid 13^2\) to divide \(a_{n+1} = 26(3^{3n + 3} -1)\). Since
\(26 = 2\cdot 13\), that product already has one factor of \(13\).
So we need another \(13\) from \(3^{3n + 3} -1\). We try,
\[3^{3n + 3} = 3^{3(n+1)} \equiv 1^{n+1} =1 \mod13\]
Thus, \(3^{3n + 3} -1 = 13m \) for some integer \(m\), and
\[2\cdot13 (3^{3n + 3} -1) = 2\cdot13(13m) = 2\cdot 169 \cdot m\]
Therefore, \(169 \mid 3^{3n + 3}-26n-27\).\qed

\section*{Question 3}
Suppose \(a,b \in \ZZ[+]\). Suppose \(\gcd(a,b) = \lcm(a,b)\). Suppose that \(d = \gcd(a,b)\).
Then \(d\geq 1\) and there exist positive integers \(a'\) and \(b'\) with \[a = d \cdot a'  \quad\text{and}\quad b = d\cdot d'\]
then we obtain \[\gcd(a',b') = 1\]
We restate GCD-LCM product formula or theorem 10 on the notes, \[\gcd(a,b) \cdot \lcm(a,b) = ab\]
Using this formula, suppose that \[d = \gcd(a,b) = \lcm(a,b)\]
Then we get, \[d \cdot d = a\cdot b \Rightarrow d^2 = (d\cdot a')(d\cdot b')\Rightarrow d^2 = d^2\cdot a'\cdot b'\]
Dividing both sides by \(d^2\), we get
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\cancelto{1}{b^2}}{\cancelto{1}{b^2}} &= \frac{\cancelto{1}{b^2}\cdot a' \cdot b'}{\cancelto{1}{d^2}}\\
    1 & = a'\cdot b'
\end{align*}
Since \(a'\) and \(b'\)are positive integers, then \(a' = 1\) and \(b' = 1\). Thus,
\[a = d \cdot a' = d\cdot1 = d \quad\text{and}\quad b = d\cdot b' = d \cdot 1 = d\]
Therefore, \(a = d = b\).\qed

\section*{Question 4}
Suppose \(p \in \ZZ[]\) and \(a\) is an arbitrary integer. We need to show that \(p \nmid a + 1\). We prove this by contradiction.
Suppose \( p \mid a +1\). Then from the definitions, we know that \(\exists m,n \in \ZZ[]\) so that 
\[a + 1 = pm \quad \text{and}\quad a = pm\]
Then, 
\begin{align*}
    ( a + 1) - a & = pm - pn \\
    1 & = pm -pn \\
    1 & = p(m  -n)
\end{align*}
Thus \(p \mid 1\). But the only way to get \(1\) is by multiplying \(1\) and \(1\). Since \(p\) is prime, then \(p >1\) so \(p \neq 1\).
Thus \(p \nmid 1 \qquad \Rightarrow\Leftarrow\). Contradiction!\\
Therefore, \(p \nmid a + 1\).\qed
